Lesson 02 · Storytelling the decision

Justify at the Right Rung

Every justification stands on something. Knowing what yours stands on — and never claiming firmer ground than you have — is the whole skill.

Time 30 minutes Win A strength-rating for any justification Builds on Lesson 01

Lesson 01 was about how you open. This one is about what happens forty seconds later, when someone says “why is it like that?” — and whether what you say next survives contact.

Most designers lose this moment in one of two ways. They under-claim, retreating into taste when they had hard evidence available. Or — far more damaging — they over-claim: they say a decision cannot be otherwise when the honest answer is that they think it's better. That one gets caught exactly once. After it, every future justification you make is discounted, including the true ones.

Why this is the promotion skill. Anyone can hold a position. A head of design is trusted to say “that one's a judgement call” about their own work — and it's precisely that willingness that makes people believe them when they say something is non-negotiable.

What a justification contains

Tom Greever's book — the one I pointed you at last time — reduces the content of any design justification to three questions. Answer these and you have said everything a stakeholder needs:1

  1. What problem does it solve?
  2. How does it affect the user?
  3. Why is it better than the alternative?

That third one is where most answers collapse. “Better than the alternative” requires you to have an alternative, held in mind, that you can describe fairly. Greever's advice is to walk into the meeting having already anticipated the objections and prepared the alternatives — showing you considered several options is what earns you the room to explain why this one won.1

But those three questions tell you what to say. They don't tell you how hard you're allowed to push. That depends on what your reason actually rests on.

The Justification Ladder

Where this comes from. The ladder is my synthesis, not a published framework — same honesty rule as last time. It reorganises the lenses Greever names (business, design, research, process)1 by how much resistance each one survives, and adds the two rungs that only exist in physical product.

Five rungs. Rung 1 ends arguments. Rung 5 starts them.

1

Physics & law

It cannot be otherwise. Material limits, moulding and process constraints, safety standards, regulatory requirement. “Below 2.5mm the tool won't fill that rib.”

Unarguable

We tested it and this happened. Field failures, warranty returns, ergonomic data, user testing. “Four of six riders couldn't operate it wearing winter gloves.”

Contestable
5

Judgement

My trained eye says so. Often the truest reason you have, and the weakest one in the room. “This proportion is right and the other one isn't.”

Costs capital

The two rules

Climb down, never up. Open at the highest rung your decision honestly stands on, then descend as needed. If there's a regulatory reason, lead with it and stop — don't decorate a rung-1 argument with taste, because you'll drag the whole thing down to rung 5 in the listener's mind.

Label rung 5 out loud. Not “this is how it must be” but “this one's my judgement, not a constraint — here's what I'd point to.” Naming it costs you the argument occasionally. It buys you something much larger: when you later say “this one genuinely cannot move,” people believe you.

The over-claim

Dressing a rung-5 preference as a rung-1 constraint is the single most expensive mistake in this lesson. It works until one engineer proposes a version that plainly does work — and then you've lost the decision and the standing to win the next five.

Before you justify anything: find out what you're answering

The stated objection is usually not the real one. “It's too expensive” can mean I don't trust your estimate, or there's no capex left this year, or I wasn't consulted and I'm reminding you I exist. Three completely different problems, one sentence. Answer the wrong one and you'll win an argument nobody was having.

Greever's counsel is to listen actively — to be genuinely working out the other person's position rather than queuing your reply — and to dig for the root motivation underneath the feedback before you respond to its surface.1 He offers a specific line for buying that time in a presentation: let them talk, thank them, then say you'll come back to all of their feedback after you've explained how you arrived at the design and why you believe it meets the need.2

So the move is four beats, and only the last one is the justification:

Pausebeat one
Let them finish completely. Then leave a beat. The silence reads as consideration, and it stops you answering the first version of the objection.
Probebeat two
One question, then stop talking. “What's the concern underneath that?” or “If we changed it, what would that fix for you?”
Placebeat three
Silently: which rung does my answer to their actual concern stand on? Pick the highest honest one.
Answerbeat four
Greever's three questions, at that rung. If the honest rung is 5, say so as you say it.

Not every decision is worth defending

The point of the meeting is agreement to move forward.2 Most of what you get pushed on doesn't matter, and conceding it cheaply and visibly — “yes, fair, let's do that” — is not weakness. It's how you accumulate the credibility you'll spend on the two decisions in the project that genuinely matter. Designers who defend everything equally are read as precious, and get overruled on the things they should have won.

The uncomfortable one

If everything on your list came out at rung 4 or 5, the decision may be weaker than your conviction about it. That's worth knowing before the meeting rather than during it — and it's usually a prompt to go and get a rung-2 reason. One afternoon of testing with six people converts a preference into evidence, and no amount of arguing does.
