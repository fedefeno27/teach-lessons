Lesson 04 · Storytelling the decision

When the Objector Outranks the Room

Seniority changes what it costs you to push. It does not change how strong your reason is — and knowing the difference is most of the skill.

Time 30 minutes Win A plan for the one review where the most senior person is against you Builds on Lesson 02 & 03

Lesson 02 gave you a probe and five levels of reason. Lesson 03 gave you a way to get a level-2 one. Both quietly assumed you were arguing with someone about your own rank. This lesson is for the review where the person saying “I don't like it” is the one who signs off the project.

Designers tend to fail this in one of two ways. They fight: they win the point in the room and lose the standing to win the next one. Or they fold: they concede everything to avoid friction and are quietly read as someone whose opinion doesn't hold weight. Both come from the same mistake — treating the meeting as a contest over the design. The way out is to change what you are asking for.

Where this comes from. I found no published method for a designer disagreeing with someone senior in a physical-product company, and I would rather tell you that than dress a guess up as a framework. The three moves below are my synthesis. They lean on Tom Greever's framing of the design meeting, as reviewers summarise it,1 and on the “disagree and commit” idea from Jeff Bezos's 2016 shareholder letter.2 Treat it as a working tool and tell me where it fails.

Rank changes the price, not the level

A director saying “the radius looks fussy” is offering a level-5 reason — their judgement, exactly as yours is when you say the radius is right. It doesn't become level 1 because of who said it. What rank changes is that they can overrule you anyway, and pushing back in front of others costs you more.

So out-arguing them is the wrong goal. Two things are worth knowing instead:

  1. They are accountable for something — margin, a launch date, the brand, a warranty exposure. A senior person's objection is often a stand-in for one of those, and the stand-in is usually the thing you can actually answer.
  2. They are not in the room to approve your design. Greever's framing, as one reviewer puts it: the purpose of the meeting is “not necessarily to get the stakeholders' approval or inputs that would change the solution. The goal for the meeting should be to gain stakeholders' support.”1 Support is a smaller thing to ask for than approval, and much easier to give.

Three moves

See them firstbefore the meeting
Fifteen minutes one-to-one, beforehand. Show them your decision sentence (Lesson 01) and ask your probe (Lesson 02): “Which is your worry — cost, date, brand or risk?” Giving them four to pick from is easier to answer than an open question. Nobody should meet a decision for the first time in front of an audience — least of all the person who can overrule it. The same reviewer's advice is to notice what a senior person fixates on and make sure it's dealt with before they see it.1
Speak their unitin the meeting
Restate the decision in what they answer for: points of margin, pounds at volume, weeks to launch, warranty exposure. This is Lesson 01 pointed upward. A sentence in their unit is one they can weigh without having to trust your taste.
Offer a step they can say yes tothe ask
Not “approve my design” but “let me bring you evidence by Thursday” (Lesson 03) or “here are two options, and my pick” (Lesson 06). It turns a verdict into a decision they can make without losing face. It only works if the step is real: dated, with the line drawn first. A step used to stall is read as stalling.

Don't go round them

If you ever have to take a decision above someone, tell them first that you're doing it and why. A senior person who hears from a third party that you escalated past them remembers it for years; one who hears it from you first usually doesn't. That is my judgement rather than a source's — but it is the kind of judgement that is expensive to test.

When you lose: disagree and commit

Sometimes the move fails and they decide against you. Bezos's version of what to do next is short: “If you have conviction on a particular direction even though there's no consensus, it's helpful to say, ‘Look, I know we disagree on this but will you gamble with me on it? Disagree and commit?’”2 He offers it as a phrase that saves time, and he uses it on himself: on one project he says he disagreed and committed.

For a designer on the losing end, that translates into three things:

  1. Say the disagreement once, at its true level. “This one's my judgement, and I'd choose the other. I'll build it your way.” Not five times, and not disguised as something firmer.
  2. Commit where people can see it. Then build it properly. A decision you quietly undermine is worse than one you lost honestly.
  3. Write the record in one line: what was decided, by whom, on what date, for what reason, and what would make you look at it again. It protects both of you, and it is how a lost argument becomes a reason to come back with evidence later.

The exception: level 1

Everything above is for levels 2 to 5. If the decision breaks a law, a safety standard or a hard process limit, it is not yours to commit to. You take it to whoever owns compliance or safety, and you tell the objector first that you're doing so. This is my inference — the letter above is about business decisions, not safety ones — but I would not want to be the designer who committed.

Read this next

Jeff Bezos, 2016 Letter to Shareholders (Amazon). Free, and short if you read only the section on making high-quality, high-velocity decisions. It is a business letter, not a design text, and it is about executives deciding — read it for the phrase and the discipline, not for advice on design reviews.2 For the meeting itself, Tom Greever's Articulating Design Decisions is still the book; the framing of a review as a request for support rather than approval is his.1
