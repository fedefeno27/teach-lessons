Lesson 06 · Storytelling the decision

Show the Trade-off, Not the Design

A single design invites a verdict: like it or don't. Two or three costed options invite a decision: which trade-off do we want?

Time 30 minutes Win A one-page options sheet that ends in a decision Builds on Lessons 01–05

The last five lessons each gave you one part of a decision: the number, the level of the reason, the evidence, the way to handle a senior objector, and the cost line. This lesson puts them on one page. It is the format in which a decision survives a room.

Bring one design to a review and the only question available is “do we like this one?” — the question where taste, rank and mood decide. Bring the same design as one of three options, each with what it costs and what we know about it, and the question becomes “which trade-off do we want?” That is the question a business is built to answer, and it is one the senior person can answer without overruling you, because you have given them something to choose.

Where this comes from. Tom Greever's advice is to walk into the meeting having anticipated the objections and prepared the alternatives1 — that is where the idea of showing options starts. The comparison method is Stuart Pugh's, in its concept-selection form.2 Ulrich and Eppinger teach a version of it for choosing between product concepts.3 The one-page sheet and the five rules are my synthesis of these with the earlier lessons. Tell me where they fail.

What would change itthe line
“Same six riders, gloved: two or more fail C and we move to B.” The line from Lesson 03, drawn in advance.
The decisionfrom whom, by when
“I need a yes on the test by Friday, from you.” A sheet that doesn't end in an ask ends in a discussion.

Five rules

  1. The current design is an option. Pugh's method puts one design in as the reference and compares the others to it as better, worse or the same on each criterion.2 It gives the room something to measure against, and it stops the current product being an invisible baseline nobody costed.
  2. Options must differ on a real trade-off. If A is obviously best and the others are there to look considered, people notice — see the pair below.
  3. Musts first. Remove any option that breaks a level-1 constraint — law, safety, a hard process limit — before comparing anything. The known weakness of scoring methods is that they tend to equalise all the requirements, so a few small advantages can outweigh a “must have”.2
  4. Show better, same, worse — don't total. Pugh's version counts the times “better” and “worse” appear for each option but does not sum them.2 A single weighted total hides the trade-off in one number, which is the opposite of what you are trying to show.
  5. Recommend, at a level, and ask. Show your pick, say which level it stands on, and finish on the decision you need. Options without a recommendation look like avoidance; a recommendation without options looks like a verdict.

Straw man

A: the design you like. B: the same design, uglier and dearer. C: something that plainly can't be made. Everyone can see who the winner is meant to be.

A real trade-off

A: today's design — costs nothing, has the known problem. B: dearer, with better evidence. C: cheapest, with none yet. Each wins on something.

Options are not a way to hide

An options sheet without a pick is a request for someone else to take the risk — and it will be read as one. Head of design is the person who says “this one, and here's how sure I am.” The sheet makes that easier to say, not unnecessary.

Read this next

Tom Greever, Articulating Design Decisions (2nd ed., O'Reilly). Still the primary source for the meeting itself, and the origin of the advice to bring prepared alternatives.1 It is mostly about software; the habits translate directly, the examples don't. For the comparison method, the concept-selection chapter of Ulrich and Eppinger's Product Design and Development is the hardware-side treatment,3 and the Wikipedia article on the decision-matrix method is a five-minute overview of Pugh's version and its known weaknesses.2
