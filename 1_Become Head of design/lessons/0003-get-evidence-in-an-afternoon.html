Lesson 03 · Storytelling the decision

Get Evidence in an Afternoon

A level-2 reason is not a research budget. It is one sharp question, six people, and a line you drew before you looked.

Time 30 minutes Win A test plan whose result you'd have to accept Builds on Lesson 02

Lesson 02 ended on an uncomfortable line: if everything on your list sits at level 4 or 5, go and get a level-2 reason. It didn't say how. This lesson does — and it assumes you have no budget, no researcher, and nobody's permission.

What stops most designers is that they picture evidence as a study. A study needs a plan, a budget and a sign-off. A level-2 reason needs much less: something that was observed rather than opined, stated at exactly the size it was observed. That can be done between lunch and the end of the day.

Where this comes from. The three sources and the four-part plan are my synthesis. They lean on Nielsen Norman Group's work on small-sample testing12 and on observation3. Those sources are about software interfaces. The logic — watch people attempt a task; few participants find the problems — carries over to a hands-on test of a physical part, but that carry-over is my inference, not theirs. Tell me if it misbehaves on real hardware.

Three places evidence hides

Cheapest first. Stop at the first one that answers your question.

Already ownedhours · free
Returns and warranty codes, service tickets, dealer or workshop notes, old test reports, supplier failure data. Someone in your company already holds it, and probably nobody has asked them about your part. Ask for a specific slice — “returns coded to this bracket, last twelve months” — not “the data”.
Test what existsone afternoon
Hand six people the current product and one task. No prototype needed: the thing you want to change is usually the best test object, because the failure you're claiming already lives in it.
Test a stand-indays
A foam block, a 3D print, a taped-up mock. Good enough when the question is size, reach, grip or force; not good enough when it is finish or feel. Ulrich and Eppinger list learning — will it work, and how well does it meet the need — among the core purposes of a prototype.5

Notice what is not on the list: asking people what they think. That is the next section.

Watch, don't ask

Nielsen's rule is blunt: “Watch what people actually do. Do not believe what people say they do. Definitely don't believe what people predict they may do in the future.”3

Ask six riders “which latch do you prefer?” and you have not collected evidence. You have collected six level-5 opinions — a poll of taste, with more people in it. Hand them the latch, in the gloves they'd really wear, say nothing, and watch. One is a claim about them; the other is a fact about your part.

What six people can and can't say

The reason six is enough is that people repeat each other. Nielsen's analysis is that five participants surface roughly 85% of the usability problems in a design, and each extra person mostly shows you what you have already seen.1 He would rather you spend a fixed budget on three small rounds of five than one round of fifteen — test, fix, test again.1 Steve Krug's do-it-yourself method is the same idea run as a habit: a morning a month, three participants per round.4

The same sources draw a hard line under it. A study this size is “aimed at collecting insights to drive your design, not numbers to impress people in PowerPoint”; if you want statistically significant numbers, the guidance is at least twenty participants, and tighter estimates need more.2

So six people can tell you that something happens. They cannot tell you how often. Your sentence has to be the size of what you saw:

Over-claimed

“Sixty-seven percent of riders can't operate the latch in winter gloves.”

Right-sized

“Six riders tried it in winter gloves; four couldn't open it first go. That's enough to say the problem is real. It isn't enough to say how common it is.”

The over-claim, again

This is Lesson 02's most expensive mistake in a lab coat. There it was a level-5 preference dressed as a level-1 constraint; here it is six people dressed as a statistic. It gets caught the same way — by the one person in the room who works with numbers — and it costs the same thing: every later result you present is discounted, including the sound ones.

The four-line plan

Every test worth running fits on four lines. Write them before you recruit anyone.

Claimline one
One sentence a test could prove false — about what people can do, not what they'd like. “Riders in winter gloves can open the new latch unaided.” Not “the new latch is better.”
Taskline two
What they will do with their hands, in one action, while you watch and don't explain. Recruit people who resemble the user and don't sit in the studio — colleagues share your eye, which is exactly the problem Lesson 01 opened with.
Lineline three · my rule
The result that would change your mind, written down before the first participant. “Two or more of six fail and we don't ship this latch.” Fix it afterwards and every result will find a way to agree with you.
Sentenceline four
How you will report it: the count, the group size, the condition — plus one clause on what it doesn't show.
In a test with [n] [who], [doing what], [k] [what happened]. That shows [it happens]. It doesn't show [how often].

A test that can't fail isn't a test

If every result you can imagine leaves your decision standing, you have built a demonstration, not evidence — level 5 with props. The line is what makes it level 2. And if it does come out against you, you have just been spared making the defence in the room.

Where it goes afterwards is where Lesson 02 already put it: you open at the highest honest level. “Four of six riders in winter gloves couldn't open it” is a level-2 opening; the pound figure from Lesson 01 then follows it, and the two together are hard to wave away.

If you can't get six people

Three is a start, and small and repeated beats big and once: test, fix what you saw, test again. That is Nielsen's argument for several small rounds over one large one,1 and it is also easier to schedule on a Thursday afternoon than a research project is.

Read this next

Jakob Nielsen, “Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users” (Nielsen Norman Group). About ten minutes, free, and the primary source for the claim this lesson leans on hardest. Read it with one caveat in mind: it is written about software interfaces and about finding problems. It is not a licence to quote percentages, and its authors say so.12 If you want the how-to version for running the sessions themselves, Steve Krug's Rocket Surgery Made Easy is the standard one.4
