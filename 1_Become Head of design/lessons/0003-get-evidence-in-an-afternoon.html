Lesson 03 · Storytelling the decision

Get Evidence in an Afternoon

A level-2 reason is not a research budget. It is one sharp claim, one measurement or one small hands-on test, and a line you drew before you looked.

Time 30 minutes Win A test plan whose result you'd have to accept Builds on Lesson 02

Lesson 02 ended on an uncomfortable line: if everything on your list sits at level 4 or 5, go and get a level-2 reason. It didn't say how. This lesson does — for a physical product, and assuming you have no budget, no researcher, and nobody's permission.

What stops most designers is that they picture evidence as a study. A study needs a plan, a budget and a sign-off. A level-2 reason needs much less: something that was measured or observed rather than opined, stated at exactly the size it was measured or observed. That fits between lunch and the end of the day.

There is a second point, and it is the one that matters at principal level. You do not have to run every test with your own hands. Engineering, quality and the test lab own the rigs. Your job is to write the claim and the line — the two things nobody else will write for you — and hand over the rest.

Where this comes from. The four places to look and the four-line plan are my synthesis; I could not find a published method for cheap hands-on testing of hardware (that gap is logged in RESOURCES.md). What I can point to is the arithmetic: how much a small number of pieces can and can't prove is standard binomial statistics, used in reliability engineering and in clinical work123, and the value of observing a product in use comes from the product-development textbook literature4. Tell me where it misbehaves on real hardware.

Four places evidence hides

Cheapest first. Stop at the first one that answers your question.

Already ownedhours · free
Returns and warranty codes, service tickets, dealer or workshop notes, supplier failure data, old test-lab reports. Someone in your company already holds it, and probably nobody has asked them about your part. Ask for a specific slice — “returns coded to this bracket, last twelve months” — not “the data”.
Measure itan hour or two
A push-pull force gauge, a scale, calipers, a tape. Operating force, weight, gap, reach, clearance — on your product and on the one you're being compared with. Measure at least three units so you can see the spread; one unit gives you a number, not a range.
Watch hands on the real thingone afternoon
Hand six people the current product and one task, in the conditions it will really meet — gloves, rain, a bag in the other hand. No prototype needed: the thing you want to change is usually the best test object, because the failure you're claiming already lives in it.
Test a stand-indays
A foam block, a 3D print, a taped-up mock. Good enough when the question is size, reach, grip or force; not good enough when it is finish or feel. Ulrich and Eppinger list learning — will it work, and how well does it meet the need — among the core purposes of a prototype.4

Notice what is not on the list: asking people what they think. That is the next section.

Watch, don't ask

Ask six riders “which latch do you prefer?” and you have not collected evidence. You have collected six level-5 opinions — a poll of taste, with more people in it. Hand them the latch, in the gloves they'd really wear, say nothing, and watch. One is a claim about them; the other is a fact about your part.

Observing a product in use is one of the three standard ways the product-development literature gathers data about what people need — alongside interviews and focus groups4 — and it is the one that shows you what the other two can only report.

What a small test can and can't say

A handful of pieces or people can be read two ways, and each has a trap.

When something fails, you have learned that it can fail. Two of six gloved riders couldn't open the latch: the problem is real, and you now know what it looks like. You do not know how many riders it affects. Two of six is a third of your sample, not a third of your customers.

When nothing fails, you have learned much less than it feels like. This is the trap that catches designers who are otherwise careful. If zero pieces fail in n trials, the true failure rate can still be well above zero. A quick rule of thumb puts the 95%-confidence upper limit at about 3 ÷ n; it was set out for medical trials1 and is a good approximation once n is above about 30.2 For small groups the exact figure is a little lower — I worked these out from the success-run formula in source 3:

Passed, none failedTrue failure rate could still be as high as
6 of 6about 39% — four in ten
10 of 10about 26% — one in four
30 of 30about 10% — one in ten

Engineers reverse the question: how many pieces must survive with no failures to claim a reliability? The standard success-run formula is n = ln(1 − confidence) ÷ ln(reliability). It gives 22 pieces for 90% reliability at 90% confidence, and 59 for 95% at 95%.3 If even one fails, the formula no longer applies.3 That is a job for the test lab — but now you know what to ask for, and why six of anything won't do.

So your sentence has to be the size of what you saw:

Over-claimed

“Six for six opened it in winter gloves, so the latch is fine for winter riders.”

Right-sized

“Six riders tried it in winter gloves; all six opened it first go. That's a good sign. It can't rule out a failure rate near four in ten, so it isn't a sign-off.”

The over-claim, again

This is Lesson 02's most expensive mistake in a lab coat. There it was a level-5 preference dressed as a level-1 constraint; here it is six clean passes dressed as a guarantee. It gets caught the same way — by the one person in the room who works with numbers — and it costs the same thing: every later result you present is discounted, including the sound ones.

The four-line plan

Every test worth running fits on four lines. Write them before you measure, recruit or book anyone.

Claimline one
One sentence a test could prove false — about what the part does or what people can do, not what they'd like. “Riders in winter gloves can open the new latch unaided.” Not “the new latch is better.”
Testline two
What you will measure, or what they will do with their hands, in one action — while you watch and don't explain. Recruit people who resemble the user and don't sit in the studio: colleagues share your eye, which is exactly the problem Lesson 01 opened with. Say who runs it: you, or the lab.
Lineline three · my rule
The result that would change your mind, written down before the first measurement. “Two or more of six fail and we don't ship this latch.” Or “any unit above 30 newtons and we change it.” Fix it afterwards and every result will find a way to agree with you.
Sentenceline four
How you will report it: the count, the group size, the condition — plus one clause on what it doesn't show.
In a test of [n] [units or people], [doing what], [k] [what happened]. That shows [it happens / it holds]. It doesn't show [how often / how long].

A test that can't fail isn't a test

If every result you can imagine leaves your decision standing, you have built a demonstration, not evidence — level 5 with props. The line is what makes it level 2. And if it does come out against you, you have just been spared making the defence in the room.

You own the claim and the line, not the rig

When the test needs equipment or hours you don't control, send engineering or quality the claim and the line with the request. It makes the ask small and specific, and it makes the result hard to argue with afterwards, because both sides agreed what would count before anyone looked. Deciding what gets tested, and what result would settle it, is design leadership work — and it is work you can do this week.

Where it goes afterwards is where Lesson 02 already put it: you open at the highest honest level. “Four of six riders in winter gloves couldn't open it” is a level-2 opening; the pound figure from Lesson 01 then follows it, and the two together are hard to wave away.

If you can't get six people or many pieces

Three is a start. Small and repeated beats big and once: test, fix what you saw, test again. Report three as three — a count and a group size — and keep the durability or reliability claims for the test lab, where 22 or 59 pieces is a normal request rather than a strange one.

Read this next

J. A. Hanley and A. Lippman-Hand, “If Nothing Goes Wrong, Is Everything All Right? Interpreting Zero Numerators” (JAMA, 1983). Three pages, free, and the origin of the 3 ÷ n rule. It is written about clinical trials, and the arithmetic is identical for a latch. One honest caveat: I could not read the paper's text in this session, so what I say about it comes from summaries — check it against the paper.1 When you want the wider view of prototypes as tools for learning, Product Design and Development by Ulrich and Eppinger is the hardware-side textbook.4
