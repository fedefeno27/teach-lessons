Lesson 01 · Executive influence & commercial fluency

Lead With the Number

How to open a design argument so that the people who decide your next job can actually act on it.

Time 30 minutes Win One reusable sentence structure Practise on A live decision at work

You already win arguments about design. The problem is where you win them — in the studio, with people who share your vocabulary. The job you want is decided by people who do not.

Here is the uncomfortable shape of the gap. A senior designer defends a decision on craft grounds and hopes the room follows. A head of design states the business consequence first, and then shows the craft that produces it. Same decision, same conviction, different order — and the second one gets promoted, because it is the only one an executive can repeat to somebody else after you leave the room.

Why this lesson first. You named five gaps. This one sits underneath three of them: commercial fluency, executive influence, and telling the story of a decision. It is also the only one you can practise this week without anyone giving you permission.

That last point is the whole mechanism. Your argument has to survive being retold by someone who was not persuaded by the render.

The three questions behind every executive nod

Whatever the meeting is nominally about, the people with budget are running your proposal against a short list. Design leadership writing converges on the same point from many directions: business fluency is what gets a design leader invited into the conversation in the first place, rather than briefed after it concludes.4

They are askingIn their wordsWhat design controls
Does this make or cost money?Margin, price point, volumeMaterial, part count, finish, assembly steps
Does this make us late?Tooling slot, launch windowHow reversible the change still is
Does this expose us?Warranty, returns, compliance, brandDurability, safety, coherence with the range

You do not need a finance qualification to use this. You need one formula and one distinction.

The one formula

Gross margin is the share of revenue left once you subtract the cost of making the thing.1

gross margin = (net sales − COGS) ÷ net sales × 100

COGS (cost of goods sold) is the direct cost of production: raw materials, direct labour, and manufacturing overhead. Administrative salaries and R&D sit outside it.12 For a physical product, the bill of materials, the tooling, and the choice of supplier set most of that number — which means a large share of COGS is decided by people holding a pen, at a stage where changing it is still cheap.

Sense of scale. Software businesses run 70–90% gross margins. Manufacturing and retail run 20–50%.1 In a 35% business, a pound off unit cost is worth roughly three pounds of extra revenue. That ratio is the single most useful thing you can carry into a review.

The one distinction

Gross profit is a figure in pounds. Gross margin is a percentage of revenue.1 Mixing them up is the tell that marks someone as visiting the commercial conversation rather than living in it. Margin is comparable across products; profit is not.

The Decision Sentence

Four slots, in this order. Say it before you show anything.

Where this comes from. The four slots are my compression, not a published framework — I want you to know which parts of this lesson are cited and which are synthesis. The underlying claim is Tom Greever's: the meeting is where design lives or dies, and how you talk about the work matters more than the work.3

Outcomeslot one
The business result, stated in a number they already track. Not "better", not "cleaner". Four pounds a unit. Six weeks earlier. Half the warranty claims.
Mechanismslot two
The design move that causes it. One clause. This is where your craft enters — as the cause of the number, not as the point.
Costslot three
What it costs in money, time, or risk — named by you, before anyone finds it. Volunteering the downside is what converts a pitch into a recommendation.
Askslot four
The specific decision you need, and by when. A proposal with no ask is a status update, and status updates do not get decided.

Worked, on a material change:

Senior designer

"I want to move the bracket to glass-filled nylon. The aluminium one has never sat right — the parting line fights the radius, and we can get a much better surface. I've done three studies and this is clearly the strongest."

Head of design

"Moving the bracket to glass-filled nylon takes about £4 out of unit cost at 40k volume, because it collapses three machined parts into one moulding. It costs us £18k of new tooling and needs a fatigue test we haven't budgeted. I need a yes or no by the 14th to hold the tooling slot."

Notice what did not happen. The second version did not abandon the craft argument — the parting line and the surface are still true, and you will still make them, on slide three, to a room that is now listening. It changed what goes first.

The failure mode to watch for

Leading with effort. "We ran three studies, modelled each one, scored them on a matrix." Effort is not an argument. It describes what the work cost you, which is the one cost in the room that nobody is deciding about.

Retrieval check

Answer from memory, not by scrolling up. Getting one wrong here is worth more than getting it right by looking — the effort of retrieval is what makes it stick.

One of three

You want to change a bracket material. Which opening line puts you in the head-of-design seat?

Two of three

"Moving the hinge inboard saves £4.10 a unit at 40k volume, and I need a decision by Friday to hold the tooling slot." Which slot is missing?

Three of three

A product sells at £100 and its COGS is £62. What is the gross margin?

Now do it on something real

Pick a decision you are actually carrying right now — something you believe is right and have not yet got agreement on. Fill the four slots. Write badly and quickly; the slots matter, the prose does not.

If you don't have the numbers

That is itself the finding, and it is worth more to you than this lesson. Being kept away from COGS is the structural reason design gets briefed late. Ask your NPI or cost engineer for the BOM breakdown on one product — framed as "I want to design to the cost target properly," which is very hard to refuse. That single request has started more design leadership careers than any portfolio.
