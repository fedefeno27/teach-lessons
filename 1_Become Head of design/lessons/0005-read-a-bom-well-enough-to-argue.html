Lesson 05 · Commercial fluency

Read a BOM Well Enough to Argue With It

Every design decision lands somewhere in the bill of materials. Learn to find where, and you can say what it costs in the finance lead's terms.

Time 30 minutes Win A defensible cost change on a real design decision Builds on Lesson 01

Lesson 01 taught the arithmetic: margin, COGS, volume. It didn't say where the numbers come from. Most of them come from one document, and designers are often the people who understand it least — even though almost every choice they make is a line in it.

A BOM — bill of materials — is the list of every part in the product: what it is, how many, what it's made from, and who supplies it. With prices attached it becomes the backbone of COGS. You don't need to become a cost engineer. You need to read it well enough to answer one question when someone says “that will cost more”: by how much, and where?

Where this comes from. The cost categories follow the chapter structure of Ulrich and Eppinger's design-for-manufacturing chapter,1 and the part-count test is the Boothroyd Dewhurst method.2 The three-pass reading and the worked example are my own. I don't yet know whether you can see your product's prices — the section below covers both cases.

Where the cost sits

Ulrich and Eppinger's design-for-manufacturing chapter breaks the job of estimating manufacturing cost into a short list: the bill of materials, the cost of standard components, the cost of custom components, the cost of assembly, and overhead — with a distinction between fixed and variable costs running through it.1 In plain words:

Where it sitsWhat it meansWhere a design decision lands
Standard partsBought off the shelf: screws, springs, bearings.Choosing a standard part over a special one; how many fasteners you specify.
Custom partsMade to your drawing: moulded, cast, machined.Material, process, finish, tolerance — and the tool that makes them.
AssemblyLabour and time to put it together.Part count, fixings, how many steps and orientations.
OverheadSupport and indirect costs allocated to the product.Mostly not yours — but complexity feeds it.

Two of those live in a different place from the rest. Variable costs are paid on every unit. Fixed costs — the tool above all — are paid once. When a tool costs £40,000 and you sell 40,000 units, it adds £1.00 to every unit — the same amortisation you met in Lesson 01. Keeping the two separate is what stops a one-off from being argued as if it were a recurring cost.

Three passes

Don't read a BOM from top to bottom. Read it three times, each for one thing.

Countpass one
How many lines? How many distinct parts? How many fasteners? How many suppliers? Nothing else. Every part is a line in the cost, a step in assembly and a possible failure — the number alone tells you how complex the product really is.
Rankpass two
Sort by cost and see what the top five lines hold, as a share of the total. In many products a few lines carry most of the cost — but check yours rather than assume it. That share tells you where an argument is worth having.
Questionpass three
For each of those top lines: standard or custom? What process makes it? Is the cost fixed or variable? Which decision of mine sets it — material, finish, shape, tolerance, or the fact that it exists at all?

The cheapest part is the one that isn't there

The most direct way a designer takes cost out is to remove a part. Boothroyd and Dewhurst's method gives a test for whether a part must be separate. It must be only if it meets at least one of three conditions:2

  1. Does it move relative to all adjacent parts during normal operation?
  2. Must it be a different material from its neighbours, for functional reasons?
  3. Must it be separate so that other parts can be assembled or taken apart?

A part that passes none of the three is a candidate for merging with a neighbour.2 That is a question, not a verdict — a merged part may need a bigger tool or a stronger material, and you have to cost both sides. The same page says every part removed takes away its material, its manufacturing, its fasteners, its assembly time and a potential failure point;2 it also quotes typical results of 40–60% fewer parts with 20–50% cost savings. That page belongs to a company that sells this method, so treat those figures as a reason to look, not as a forecast for your product.

From a line to an argument

Here is a small BOM, with invented numbers for the arithmetic — yours come from the real one.

LineQtyTypeMade by£ per unit
Housing1CustomInjection moulding3.20
Cover1CustomInjection moulding1.40
Screws2StandardBought in0.10
Hinge pin1StandardBought in0.35
Spring1StandardBought in0.20
Latch1CustomDie casting2.10
Assembly—LabourIn-house0.90
Total8.25

The cover screws to the housing, never moves against it, is the same material, and needs no service access. It fails all three tests. Suppose the merged housing costs £0.80 more (a bigger tool, more material), the cover and screws disappear, and assembly saves £0.30. The net change per unit is +0.80 − 1.40 − 0.10 − 0.30 = −1.00: a pound off every unit.

Now turn it into a Lesson 01 sentence:

Merging the cover into the housing takes £1.00 out of every unit — £40k a year at 40,000 units — for a one-off £12k on the tool.

That is a claim finance can check: a per-unit change, a volume, a one-off, and the payback (£12k against £40k a year is about four months). One rule for using it: until purchasing or the supplier has quoted, say “estimated” and say who the estimate is from. An estimate presented as a quote is a small over-claim — the same mistake as in Lesson 02, in pounds.

If you can't see the prices

Two steps are still open to you. First, get the structure without the prices — the part list, quantities, materials and processes usually sit with engineering, and pass one (count) needs nothing else. Second, ask finance or purchasing for something narrow: cost by category, or just the top five lines. A designer who has never seen the product's cost is common; asking a specific, small question is how you find out whether that's an accident or a policy. Either way, the answer is worth knowing.
