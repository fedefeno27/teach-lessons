The X-Ray

A fund's name tells you what it is called. Only the look-through tells you what you own — and yours is not what you think.

You told me the Europe overweight is deliberate, and I believe you. Here is the problem: a decision to overweight Europe and the portfolio that resulted from it are two different objects, and nobody had ever put them side by side. This lesson gives you the tool that does — then points it at your own eleven funds.

One thing to be clear about

Nothing here is a recommendation to sell anything. The purpose of an X-ray is to make every bet in a portfolio explicit and sized, so that the ones you want to keep are kept on purpose. What you do afterwards is your call, and you will make it better with the numbers in front of you.

The whole method is one line

Sector and country exposures do not live at the fund level. They live at the portfolio level, and you get them by weighting:

exposure = Σ ( portfolio_weight × exposure_within_fund )

This is the same arithmetic a fund manager's risk system runs, just with fewer rows. The reason it feels advanced is not that the maths is hard — it is that almost nobody bothers to do it, so almost nobody knows what they own.

A fund that is 17% of your portfolio and 30% financials contributes 0.17 × 0.30 = 5.1% of financials to the total. Do that for every holding, add them up, and you have the real number. Two rules make the output trustworthy:

  1. Include every fund, not just the obvious ones. The exposure you miss is always in the fund you did not think of as a sector fund. Dividend screens and regional trackers are where it hides.
  2. Express the result as a share of equity, not of the portfolio. Gold and cash dilute a sector percentage without reducing the underlying bet by a single share. A figure measured against the whole portfolio will always flatter you.

Roughly 40% of your equity is financials, against 17% for the world. That is about 2.3 times the global weight — and around sixty per cent of it comes from one 49-stock fund.

Push it one level further and it sharpens. Much of that exposure is not "financials" in the broad sense of insurers, exchanges and asset managers. It is banks: 49 European banks in X7PP, Australian banks arriving through PAXG (Australia is 58.7% of that index, and its financial sector is dominated by the big four lenders), and more banks arriving through the dividend screen, because high dividend yield is a screen that selects banks almost by construction.

Intent versus implementation

This is the concept worth taking away, and it generalises far beyond your portfolio.

What you decidedWhat the portfolio implements
Overweight Europe
A bet on European economies, spread across their whole listed market		 Overweight European banks
A bet on interest rate margins, credit losses and bank balance sheets

Those are not variations of the same idea. European banks can fall hard in a year when European equities rise, and the reverse. If the conviction is "Europe is undervalued relative to the US", the instrument that expresses it is a broad European index. If the conviction is specifically "European banks are undervalued and rates will stay favourable", then X7PP is the right tool — but that is a much narrower, much more expert claim, and it should be sized like one.

Worth knowing about the same fund: X7PP is swap-based (synthetic), Irish-domiciled, TER 0.20%, accumulating, 49 holdings. Not a problem in itself — Invesco's swap range is well regarded and synthetic replication is sometimes the superior structure. But an 18% position in a swap-based fund should be a decision, not a discovery. That is Lesson 1's point arriving in your own account. Source: justETF fund profile, IE00B5MTWD60.

The question this lesson asks you to answer is not "is this bad?" It is: which of those two rows did you mean? Only you can say. But you can no longer say you did not know which one you own.

Nearly two thirds of your equity is in active bets wearing index clothing. Every one of them is a passive fund in the technical sense — rules-based, transparent, cheap by historic standards. None of them is passive in the meaningful sense, because each one has chosen to hold something other than the market.

And even "broad" flatters the first row. PAXG is a regional market-cap index, so it earns the label — but it is 58.7% Australia and 49.4% financials, which makes it a good deal more concentrated than the word suggests. There is no fund anywhere in either sleeve that simply owns global equities in global proportions. That absence — the missing core — is the structural headline, and it is where Lesson 3 begins.

Retrieval check

Four questions

From memory. Options shuffle on every load, so position tells you nothing.

  1. A fund is 17.4% of your portfolio and is itself 30% financials. What does it contribute to your total financials exposure?

  2. You decide to overweight Europe and implement it with a European bank sector fund. What have you actually bet on?

  3. Why express a sector figure as a share of the equity sleeve rather than of the whole portfolio?

  4. IESU (S&P 500 Energy) sits alongside VUAG (S&P 500) in the same portfolio. What does holding IESU actually do?
