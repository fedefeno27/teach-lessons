What an ETF Really Costs You

The fee on the tin is the one cost you can be certain you are not paying. Learn to find the number that actually happened.

You already own ETFs. If you chose them the way almost everyone does, you compared the headline charge and picked the cheaper one. That instinct is right in spirit and wrong in execution — and over the eighteen-year horizon you are actually investing across, the gap between the two is worth more than most people's annual contributions.

This lesson gives you one skill: take any ETF and determine what owning it has genuinely cost, rather than what its factsheet advertises. It is the foundation for everything that follows, because there is no point weighting a portfolio built from leaky instruments. Weighting comes in Lesson 2. First, the instruments.

The number on the tin

The TER — Total Expense Ratio, sometimes shown as OCF, Ongoing Charges Figure — is the annual charge the manager discloses. It is a real number and a legally meaningful one. It is also, structurally, incomplete.

The TER appears in the "Charges" section of every fund's Key Information Document. It captures management fees and most fixed operating costs. It does not capture what the fund spends trading, what it loses to tax, or what it earns lending stock. Sources: ETF.com, Morningstar.

Four things the TER leaves out, each of which can move your return by more than the TER itself:

Transaction costs inside the fund
Every time the index rebalances, the fund trades. Those spreads and commissions are paid out of your money and sit outside the ongoing charge.
Cash drag
Dividends arrive on one date and get reinvested on another. In between they earn deposit rates while the index assumes they are fully invested.
Withholding tax
The fund is taxed on its own dividend income before you see any of it. How much depends on where the fund is legally domiciled — see section 3. This is invisible in every fee table ever published.
Securities lending income
Funds lend holdings to short sellers for a fee. This is a negative cost, and it is why some funds beat their own stated charge.

The number that actually happened

Tracking difference is the fund's return minus the index's return, annualised over a stated period. It is an outcome, not an estimate — it nets every item above, disclosed or not.

Sign convention — fix yours now

Sources disagree on which way this number runs. This workspace uses fund minus index, so a negative tracking difference means the fund lagged its index. Some sites, including several popular aggregators, invert it so that positive means underperformance. Always check which way a table runs before you compare two funds from two sources — this is a genuinely common way to reach a confidently wrong conclusion.

Figures illustrative, chosen to make the mechanism visible. Real spreads between funds on the same index are usually narrower than this — but a gap of 10–15 basis points between well-known funds tracking identical indices is entirely ordinary, and it is invisible if you only read the TER.

Fund A advertises a charge roughly one third of Fund B's, and cost you nearly four times as much. Nothing here is a trick: A is simply running a less efficient operation, and B is recapturing costs through lending revenue and tax handling. The TER told you about intent. The tracking difference told you about result.

Why a fund can beat its own benchmark

Occasionally you will see a positive tracking difference — the fund outran the index it tracks. This looks like an error and usually is not. Two legitimate mechanisms:

Most UCITS equity funds track a Net Total Return index, which calculates dividends after deducting an assumed withholding tax — conventionally the full 30% US statutory rate. An Irish fund only actually suffers 15%. It therefore keeps a slice of dividend income the benchmark assumed was lost, and beats the benchmark structurally. Source: TrackingDifferences.com; Bogleheads wiki.

  1. Securities lending revenue exceeds the fund's running costs. Real income, real counterparty risk, disclosed in the annual report.
  2. The benchmark assumes worse tax treatment than the fund receives. This is the more important one, and it leads directly to the next section.

The invisible tax: domicile

Where a fund is legally registered determines the withholding tax it pays on its own dividend income. This is charged at the fund level, before any distribution or reinvestment, and it appears in no fee table anywhere.

Domicile US dividend withholding Why
Ireland IE… 15% Irish funds are structured so as to access the US–Ireland tax treaty rate.
Luxembourg LU… 30% Luxembourg has a US treaty, but its reduced rate does not extend to SICAVs — the standard UCITS vehicle. The default statutory rate applies.

The ISIN's first two letters give the domicile away instantly: IE Ireland, LU Luxembourg, FR France. You can check any holding you own in about five seconds without opening a single document. Source: ETF Stream — Ireland vs Luxembourg.

Put a number on it. The drag is market weight × dividend yield × withholding rate. For a fund holding US equities yielding roughly 1.05%, the Ireland–Luxembourg difference is:

1.00 × 1.05% × (30% − 15%)  =  0.16% per year

S&P 500 dividend yield was approximately 1.06% in August 2026 — historically low, which currently understates this effect relative to its long-run average of around 1.6%. Re-check the live figure rather than memorising this one. Source: GuruFocus, S&P 500 dividend yield.

Sixteen basis points a year, permanently, from a single line in a legal document — larger than the entire TER of most mainstream index funds. This is what "advanced" actually means in practice: not exotic strategies, but noticing the costs that are structural and invisible rather than advertised and small.

Why this is UK-shaped

Under the UK PRIIPs regime, retained after Brexit, US-domiciled ETFs — VTI, VOO, SPY — are effectively unavailable to UK retail investors. Your universe is UCITS, and within it domicile is a choice you are actively making whether or not you realise it. Most ETF writing online is American; read it for method and ignore its tickers.

What 45 basis points does over your horizon

Cost differences feel trivial because they are quoted in fractions of a percent. Compounding is what converts them into money.
