Fund Forensics

Ten fields to pull from any ETF before it enters your portfolio — where each one hides, what good looks like, and what should stop you.

Every fund publishes far more than a fee. This card is the extraction routine: ten fields, each with a source you can open in a browser, so that judging a fund becomes a repeatable procedure instead of a feeling.

Fields 1–4 are structural — they tell you what the thing is. Fields 5–7 are cost — what owning it takes from you. Fields 8–10 are durability and tax — whether it survives and how HMRC sees it. Work in that order; a structural failure means you never need to price the thing.

Structure

FIELD 01

Domicile

The country whose law the fund lives under. It sets the withholding tax the fund suffers on its own dividends before you see a penny — a cost that never appears in the TER.

Where KID header, factsheet "Domicile", or the first two letters of the ISIN: IE = Ireland, LU = Luxembourg, FR = France. Good IE for anything holding US equities: the US–Ireland treaty gives the fund a 15% rate on US dividends. Flag LU on a US-heavy fund: SICAVs generally miss the treaty rate and suffer the full 30%.
FIELD 02

Replication method

Physical (the fund owns the shares — fully, or a sampled subset) or synthetic (the fund owns collateral and swaps its return for the index return with a bank).

Where KID, "Objectives and Investment Policy". The word to hunt for is swap. Good Physical full replication for large liquid indices. Synthetic is legitimate and sometimes tax-superior on US indices — but know which you hold. Flag Synthetic with a single counterparty and thin collateral disclosure. UCITS caps counterparty exposure at 10% of assets; check the fund actually reports against it.
FIELD 03

Distribution policy

Accumulating funds reinvest dividends inside the fund. Distributing funds pay them to you. Inside an ISA or SIPP this is a convenience and rebalancing question, not a tax one.

Where Fund name suffix — (Acc), (C) for accumulating; (Dist), (D), (Inc) for distributing. Confirm on the KID; the suffix is a convention, not a rule. Good Accumulating while building, for fewer moving parts and no idle cash. Distributing once the portfolio funds spending. Flag Holding both share classes of one fund by accident — a classic symptom of buying without a system.
FIELD 04

Index tracked — the exact name

"S&P 500" is not an index specification. Net Total Return, Gross Total Return and Price versions of the same index differ by the whole dividend stream and its assumed tax. Comparing a fund to the wrong variant makes tracking data meaningless.

Where Factsheet, "Benchmark". Look for NTR, Net TR, GTR, Price Return. Good Net Total Return — the honest benchmark, and the one most UCITS funds quote against. Flag Two funds you are comparing quoting against different index variants. Normalise before drawing any conclusion.

Cost

FIELD 05

TER / OCF

The disclosed annual charge. An input to cost, not a measure of it: it excludes transaction costs inside the fund, cash drag, withholding tax, and securities lending income.

Where KID, "Charges" — "ongoing charges". Good Use it to build a shortlist, never to settle a decision. Flag A TER that recently dropped. Fee cuts are often funded by changes elsewhere; check tracking difference held up.
FIELD 06

Tracking difference, over 3–5 years

Fund return minus index return, annualised. The outcome metric — it nets everything, including the items the TER omits. This is the number that decides what a fund cost you.

Where Provider factsheet (fund vs benchmark, multiple periods), or an aggregator such as trackingdifferences.com, whose data comes from KIIDs and monthly factsheets. Good A small, stable gap across several years. Stability matters more than any single year's figure. Flag A gap much wider than the TER, or one that jumps around year to year. Both suggest operational drag you are paying for and cannot see.

Sign convention: sources disagree. Fix yours and state it. This workspace uses fund minus index, so negative means the fund lagged. Some sites invert it. Always check which way a table runs before comparing.

FIELD 07

Bid–offer spread

The gap between buy and sell price — a cost you pay on every purchase, so it compounds with contribution frequency, not with holding period.

Where Broker order screen, or the fund page on justETF. Good A few basis points on a large mainstream fund, quoted during London hours when the underlying market is also open. Flag Wide or erratic spreads, and trading a fund whose underlying market is shut — spreads widen when market makers are hedging blind.

Durability & tax

FIELD 08

Fund size (AUM) and age

Small funds get closed. Closure is not a catastrophe — you are returned your money — but it forces a sale on someone else's timetable and can crystallise a taxable event outside a wrapper.

Where Factsheet, "Fund size" / "Net assets". Good Comfortably into the hundreds of millions, with a multi-year record. Larger funds also tend to have tighter spreads and more market makers. Flag Under roughly £100m and shrinking, or a fund launched into a theme in the last eighteen months.
FIELD 09

Securities lending policy

Many funds lend their holdings to short sellers for a fee. That income improves tracking difference; it also introduces counterparty and collateral risk. It is a trade, not free money.

Where Prospectus, annual report, or the provider's securities lending disclosure page. Good A disclosed cap on the share on loan, over-collateralisation, and most of the revenue returned to the fund rather than the manager. Flag No disclosure at all, or a large slice of lending revenue retained by the manager.
FIELD 10

UK Reporting Fund Status

An HMRC election. With it, gains on an offshore fund are taxed as capital gains; without it, as income at up to 45%. Irrelevant inside an ISA or SIPP — and decisive the moment you hold anything outside one.

Where HMRC's published list of approved reporting funds, updated monthly; also stated on most provider tax pages. Good Reporting status held continuously. Most mainstream Ireland-domiciled UCITS ETFs have it. Flag Buying an obscure fund into a taxable account without checking. This is the single most expensive unforced error available to a UK investor.

The arithmetic

What a fund costs you to hold

// annual, and the only holding-cost number that matters
annual_cost ≈ −(tracking_difference)

// example: TD of −0.19%/yr means the fund cost you 0.19%/yr,
// whatever its TER happened to say

What a fund costs you to buy

// per purchase — scales with how often you trade, not how long you hold
entry_cost ≈ (spread ÷ 2) + broker_commission

// monthly contributions pay this twelve times a year;
// this is why free regular-investment dealing is worth real money

Withholding tax drag from domicile

wht_drag = market_weight × dividend_yield × withholding_rate

// Ireland vs Luxembourg on a pure US equity fund:
// difference = 1.00 × yield × (0.30 − 0.15)
// at a ~1.05% US yield that is ≈ 0.16%/yr — larger than many TERs

Is switching worth it?

breakeven_years = switching_cost ÷ annual_saving

switching_cost = sell_spread + buy_spread + commissions
// + capital gains tax, if and only if you are outside a wrapper

// inside an ISA or SIPP there is no tax leg, so the bar is low —
// but a saving under ~0.10%/yr rarely justifies the disruption

The five-minute routine

  1. Open the KID. Read domicile, replication, distribution policy. Roughly sixty seconds, and it eliminates most candidates.
  2. Open the factsheet. Take the exact benchmark name, fund size, and the fund-vs-benchmark return table.
  3. Compute the gap over three and five years. Compare it to the TER. If the gap is much wider, ask why before you buy.
  4. Check the spread on your broker's order screen during London hours.
  5. Check for duplication against what you already own — same index, same region, same top ten holdings. Overlap is the cost nobody prices.
UK note

US-domiciled ETFs — VTI, VOO, SPY — are effectively unavailable to UK retail investors under the UK PRIIPs regime, which was retained after Brexit. Most US-written ETF advice is therefore inapplicable here. Read it for method; ignore its tickers.
